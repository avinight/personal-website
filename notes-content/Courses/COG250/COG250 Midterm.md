- [[The naturalistic imperative]] of cognitive science
    
    Analyze, Formalize, Mechanize. These three imperatives accomplish the goal of explaining cognition using our current knowledge of the mind. We analyze by breaking down observations in the universe into their fundamental component parts. Formalizing a theoretical construct requires developing plausible evidence to use in all domains of cognitive science. Mechanizing requires taking the design stance in order to make cognition, and only then will we have understood the mind.
    
    This imperative is a theoretical framework for how the field of science may be unified by including us, the creators, inside its worldview. Rigorous theoretical debate and experimental competition, as is with all good science, contribute greatly to creating properly formalizable theories that substantiate a grand unification for all sciences. Generalizing cognition to mathematics and conversely, explaining mathematics in terms of the mind may generate a full understanding of cognition.
    
    One might wonder that since the criteria for satisfying the naturalistic imperative is outlined by concepts that were made using the mind if these three imperatives are themselves, homuncular in nature.
    
    We must satisfy the naturalistic imperative without using homuncular reasoning. The two imperatives: formalize and mechanize, are the hardest to satisfy without avoiding the homunculus.
    
- [[Homuncular Fallacy]]
    
    Fallacious reasoning and argumentation often arise due to easily missed illogical blunders. Implementing an explanation in terms of itself results in the inductive problem of homunculus. Like concepts, definitions are prone to the homuncular problem. i.e. Metaphors are filled with metaphors.
    
    As the homuncular fallacy pertains to cognition, we need to formalize a construct that explains the mind, which means avoiding this fallacy. Induction in this case explains the mind in terms of a little man inside the mind who also has a mind. The problematic nature of this fallacy means that no first principle exists to base our claims about cognition. An axiomatic process that avoids the homuncular fallacy would truly explain cognition.
    
    In a perfect world, assuming the position that representations of an idea are subjective would guarantee a non-homuncular argument. Though this assumption is logical, it completely circumvents the naturalistic imperative. We want to explicate why we find things obvious, not avoid them.
    
    When we take things too seriously, the homuncular fallacy may blind us from developing plausible constructs. Establishing a plausible construct should require freeing all lines of inference of the fallacy.
    
- [[Plausibility]]
    
    Plausibility means either highly probable or very reasonable to say. Highly probable constructs denote a statistical likelihood for that construct to be true. Reasonable to say constructs denote when the mapping to and from that construct is made clear, taken seriously, and is sensibly justified. We must hence be careful to avoid equivocating on this word. 
    
    Intermodal information involves creating constructs that have multiple lines of inference towards those constructs that ultimately explain how we do science. More often than not, an imbalance in these lines of inference impedes scientific progress. Farfetchedness uses little lines of inference to produce high elegance. Triviality has high convergence, yet produces little elegance. Only by having a balance of significant proportion to the lines of inference produce profound knowledge.
    
    Deepities are constructs that look profound but are the result of equivocation between a trivial construct and a farfetched one. Avoiding deepities are difficult to identify, but necessary to validate some construct.
    
    Theories of categorization are constructs that we need to determine are profound. Often, some shared assumption presupposes the theories we make. Presuppositions are closely related to plausibility in that they offer knowledge which we can use to generate new theories.
    
- [[Classical Theory]]
    A concept is a mental definition that denotes a set of individually necessary and jointly sufficient features and that all features are relevant to the concept. The classical theory states that all concepts have essences. 
    
    This theory attempts to formalize the Similarity ⇒ Categorization problem by including necessary and sufficient conditions. We gain an advantage over the resemblance theory because it explains the productivity and compositionality of concepts. Given a list of features, we are able to group them together to create concepts and describe existing ones. 
    
    Homuncular arguments begin revealing themselves the more we trust the classical theory. Sets of features must have been generated from somewhere. As we see, not all categories have essences as they are partially dependent on context. Consequently, the classical theory fails to explain transitivity because feature lists are finite. We may also run into the [[Essentialism Bias]] dilemma where on one hand, no categories have essences, and on the other, we know that math and science discover that there are essences. 
    
    Micro-Theory, where no category has essences. Avoids making similarity judgments. It employs a structural-functional organization of concepts which the classical theory lacks.
    
- [[Micro-Theory]]
    We can explain all concepts using micro-theories; an adaptive, gestalt set of features based on anticipating the future that incorporates fuzzy boundaries to produce concepts. 
    
    Formalizing and mechanizing cognition this way seems possible because of the implementation of fuzzy logic, that retains the necessary and sufficient conditions from the classical theory. Explaining categorization in terms of its structure and function is useful for identifying behavior and interaction for membership to a category. Linear separability in data generated from decision procedure for whether to include an object in a category (membership)
    
    Micro-Theory does not answer the question of how our intuitions form explanations. We cannot explain what an explanation is because coming up with an explanation to explain scientific explanations is circular. 
    
    This theory supports Synoptic integration in a way that it tries to explain cognition in an interdisciplinary sense. For example, A micro theory of concepts can apply to Anthropology to explain "uncleanliness" by having mutually explanatory relations between different micro-theories.
    
- Synoptic Integration
    
    Synoptic Integration is the ideal view of cognitive science. Cognitive Scientists believe that establishing connections between the disciplines by which we can easily transfer and understand knowledge and discourses afford profound development to the way cognitive science is practiced.
    
    The way we do cognitive science attempts to finish the history of scientific revolutions, but we cannot achieve this when so many views of cognitive science oppose Synoptic Integration. General Nominalism and Interdisciplinary Eclecticism are both flawed views of cognitive science. The former rejects bridging and says that all disciplines are disconnected from each other, which puts the field in danger. The latter is a transitionary, yet unstable view of whetting their appetite to the field. 
    
    Common pitfalls of Synoptic Integration include ignorance and not having a thorough understanding of all the disciplines when most people only specialize in one. Unless we build profound interest in bridging the gaps in people's minds, reconfiguring them remains difficult and makes them susceptible to fragmenting the disciplines. 
    
    The naturalistic imperative is the process that manufactures the mechanisms which make cognitive science applicable to the real world.
