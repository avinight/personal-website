# 1.1 Functions of Categorization

- The major functions are coding of experience and licensing of inferences

## 1.1.1 Coding of Experience

- We code by ***concept***
    - (e.g.) chair = place to sit on ⇒ a complex object
    - fundamental to mental life because of reduced demand load on the brain
        - Perceptual processes, storage space, and reasoning processes
- Taxonomy = a hierarchy in which successive levels refer to increasingly general kinds of objects.
- Concepts at an intermediate level are more likely to be used to code experience than those at higher and lower levels
    - Fruit - High level
    - Apple - Intermediate "basic" level
    - Macintosh Apple - low "subordinate" level
    
    Facts of preference
    
    1. people prefer 'apple' to 'fruit' or 'Macintosh Apple'
    2. people can decide that a particular apple is an apple faster than they can decide that it is a 'fruit' or 'mac'

## 1.1.2 Inductive Inferences

- There's a distinction between 'deductive' and 'inductive' inferences
    - Deductive inferences are perceived as being "impossible" to disprove the new belief if the old one is True.
    - Inductive inferences are perceived as being "improbable" to disprove the new belief if the old one is True.
- Categorizing an object licenses inductive inferences about an object.
    - (e.g.) round reddish object → apple? ⇒ edible and has seeds
    - Our senses inform us and using all of our senses strengthens our knowledge about the thing we're observing.
    - Categorization is the mental means we have for inferring invisible properties from visible ones.
    - Membership in the same category means two objects have the same hidden properties
- Gelman and Markman (1986) experiment
    - three pictures: 1 bat with a left aortic arch, 1 bird with a right aortic arch, third picture looked like one of the first two but was from the other category.
    - If presupposed with the idea that it's a bird, they will say a bird.
    - If presupposed with the idea that it's a bat, they will say a bat.
    - Decisions are based on common category membership > physical similarity
- The lower the level of categories, the more inductive inferences are made
- People are more likely to make inductive inferences about invisible properties for natural kinds than artefacts.
- Classes of objects aren't treated as a category, presumably because the objects in these classes have few salient properties in common. They are not assumed to share invisible properties

## 1.2.2 Measurement of Similarity

### 1.2.2.1 Geometric Approach ← I don't like this approach

- Objects are represented as points in an n-dimensional space where the distance is the dissimilarity between the two items.
    - Why doesn't this work? Two apples are functionally similar, but they're clearly different in appearance. They aren't the same object. If they were the same object, then they would be the "same" point in space. But one apple isn't two apples and two apples aren't one apple.
    - Do these properties conform to our dimensional reality?
- Axioms for a mathematical metric for a psychological distance of similarity
    1. Minimality (Distance between points are minimal as possible)
        
        $d(a, b) \leq d(a, a)=d(b,b)=0$
        
    2. Symmetry (Distance between points is the same)
        
        $d(a,b)=d(b,a)$
        
    3. Triangle inequality (The shortest distance between two points is a line)
        
        $d(a, b) +d(b, c) \geq d(a, c)$
        
- These axioms are rebutted:
    - Minimality is compromised since the more we know about an item, the more similar it is judged to itself
        - (e.g.) A familiar category like apples seems more similar to itself than an unfamiliar category like pomegranates.
        - Why bother judging something to itself if they're the same object?
    - Symmetry is undermined because an unfamiliar category is judged more similar to a familiar one than the other way around.
        - (e.g.) *Pomegranate* is judged more similar to *apple* than *apple* is to *pomegranate*
            - I don't think this is true, on the exterior they are both similar, there is no preference or asymmetry on judgement being made. This is just biased.
    - Triangle Inequality
        - (e.g.) Jamaica is similar to Cuba, and Cuba is similar to North Korea, but Jamaica is dissimilar to North Korea.
            - They are all *countries,* therefore all of them should be similar?

# 1.3 Similarity and Verbal Categorization

## 1.3.1 Typicality Effects

- Rating predicts how efficiently people can categorize instances in a verbal categorization task.
    - (e.g.) similarity decision speed test ← Why is this a good measure for categorization when people's times are different? There will be outliers given a data set of people that did the test.
- A child developing languages acquires the names of typical category members before those of atypical ones
    - (e.g.) Fireman and Police before Paramedic or Financial Consultant

## 1.3.2 Typicality as Similarity

- Similarity in Contrast model? Steps:
    1. Select a domain of instances
    2. Estimate the features of the instances and the category (these features comprise the instance representations and concept).
    3. Apply the contrast model to each instance-concept pair.
    4. See whether this estimate of instance-concept similarity correlates with the rated typicality of the instance in the category
- What's wrong with *the prototype representation*: The contrast model tells us salient features of a category rather than what features are essential for that category.
- Assumptions made from a simplified version of the contrast model:
    1. All features are equally salient.
    2. Common and Distinctive features count equally.
- Model generalization of categorization:
    1. The representations of the target category and test item are permanently stored in long-term memory, but once activated they also become part of working memory, where all subsequent processing ensues.
    2. An item will be categorized as an instance of a category if and only if its representation exceeds some criterial level of similarity to the concept associated with that category.
        1. (More typical items will be categorized faster)
    3. The time needed to determine that an item exceeds this criterial level of similarity is less the more the item is similar to the concept.
        1. (More typical items will be categorized faster)
- Possible implementation of contrast model:
    - Assume all features of the instance and concept are compared in parallel with common features incrementing a similarity counter and distinctive features decrementing it
    - Outcomes of these feature comparisons become available at different times
- Approach #2:
    - *spreading activation*
    - When an item representation and concept are activated, the activation from these two sources begins to spread to the features associated with them.
        - What if I had no prior knowledge of the said object and assumed features based on the name of an object and then created a visual representation using previous mental connections made to that string of characters?

# 1.4 Similarity and Visual Categorization

- Instead of categorizing words, people categorize pictures/drawings/object of objects.
- Categorization times decrease with the typicality of test item.
- *excitatory/inhibitory connection:* existing arrows made from connecting two nodes in a neural-type network.
    1. excitatory: results in an interconnected network with the property that once some of a concept's features are activated, the rest also become activated.
    2. inhibitory: results in a network where the more typical the instance of the target, the higher the overall level of activation.

## 1.4.1 Importance of Shape

- Features won't work because they won't manifest the concept into the actual object being categorized. They are just words.
- Object recognition: we rely heavily on detailed information about the shape of an object

## 1.4.2 Typicality as Shape Similarity

- Detailed representation of shapes:
    1. People form a mental representation of the shape of the test object and determine its similarity to the shape information in the target concept.
    2. Only if the similarity in shape exceeds some criterion do people decide that the test item is a member of the target category.
    3. The more typical of its category an object is rated, the more similar its shape is to those of other category members, and to the shape information in any prototype of the category
    4. The more similar an instance is to its category, the faster and more accurately it can be categorized.

# 1.5 Breakdowns of Verbal and Visual Categorization

- *agnosias -* breakdowns with certain forms of brain damage
    - Temporal lobe damage

## 1.5.1 Category-Specific Deficits

- *prosopagnosia -* selective loss of ability to categorize/recognize faces

## 1.5.2 Two Hypotheses about Category-Specific Deficits

1. The structural-similarity hypothesis:
    - Superordinate, natural-kind categories tend to be more similar in shape than are instances of superordinate, artifact categories
    - Assumptions
        - The categorization of visual objects first considers just the shape representation
        - If the shape representation is associated with more than one object, consideration of shape will not provide a unique categorization of the test object, and additional processing of the details of the shape will be needed.
        - This additional processing is particularly vulnerable to the effects of brain damage.
    - Best advice: if categories are divided on the basis of whether or not their members are similar in shape to one another, the categories with similar members are associated with larger deficits in brain-damaged patients
2. The perceptual-functional hypothesis:
    - Deficit is at the level of prototypical features rather than shape descriptions
    - Key Ideas
        - Categorizing objects depends on prototypical features, some of which refer to perceptual aspects and some of which refer to functional aspects
        - Mainly perceptual features are included in the representation of natural kinds, whereas functional as well as perceptual features figure in the representation of artifacts
        - Perceptual features are particularly vulnerable to the effects of brain damage

Implications

- Support proposal that the representation of a category includes shape descriptions as well as prototypical features.
- SSH subsequent process may deal with the details of the shape
- PFH shows importance of distinguishing between the two types of prototypical features
- Research with brain damaged patients ⇒ categorization at neurological level
    - Visual: inferior part of temporal lobes, possible verbal
    - Verbal: frontal lobe
